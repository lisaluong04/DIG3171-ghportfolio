CoPilot:
The JSTOR article “Text-Mining Short Fiction by Zora Neale Hurston and Richard Wright using Voyant Tools” by Kenton Rambsy explores how digital humanities methods, specifically the text-analysis platform Voyant Tools, can be used to study African American literature in new ways. Rather than relying solely on traditional close reading, Rambsy demonstrates how computational analysis can quantify language patterns, dialect usage, word density, and stylistic differences in selected short stories by Zora Neale Hurston and Richard Wright.

Prompt: What are some key takeaways?

CoPilot:
Key Findings
1. Hurston and Wright Use AAVE Differently

The analysis shows that both writers employ vernacular speech, but they favor different lexical forms. For example:

Wright frequently uses forms such as“yuh,” “whut,” and “don.”
Hurston often uses“dat,” “An',” and “ef.”

These differences suggest that although both writers depict Southern Black communities, they construct vernacular language in distinct ways.

2. Hurston Displays Greater Lexical Diversity

Voyant's word-density measures indicate that Hurston's stories contain a wider variety of words and phrases than some of Wright's works. Rambsy argues that this diversity results partly from Hurston's technique of blending narrator voice and community dialogue, allowing many characters to contribute unique expressions and speech patterns.

A particularly interesting observation is that minor characters in Sweat contribute substantially to the story's pool of unique words, increasing linguistic richness.

3. Wright's Style Changes Within Stories

The article finds that Wright's language becomes more varied when he moves into a character's interior thoughts. In Big Boy Leaves Home, sections focused on Big Boy's consciousness show greater linguistic diversity and a richer mixture of Standard English and AAVE than earlier sections. Text-mining reveals these shifts quantitatively.

4. Literary Categories Can Be Reconsidered

The software also highlights substantial differences in story length. For example, Big Boy Leaves Home contains far more words than Spunk, even though both are categorized as short stories. Rambsy uses this finding to question assumptions about genre classifications and encourage more precise analysis of literary forms.

This is my first time utilizing Voyant and it was definitely a learning curve. To my understanding, it analyzes the entirety of text and breaks it down word for word (literally) like the total word count, frequently used words, and word trends relative to each other. It uncovers any hidden themes that readers typically overlook when simply reading the text. It doesn't let you figure out the actual context of the text, so you can't input the text for analyzing without actually reading it or else you will not know what the text is about. For future research, the ability to paste in a URL or block of text and immediately generate visualization is a helpful tool for distant reading. However, as mentioned, close reading and critical analysis is still required to fully understand the scope of the corpus.

With CoPilot, you are able to ask it anything and it will generate a response pulling from anywhere on the internet. Since CoPilot runs directly in your browser or text editor, it can complete tasks like summarizing emails, draft papers, and auto complete code, which is a helpful tool when you need to get things going, like research. On the other hand, when prompted with long documents, large codebases, or multi-level historical archives, CoPilot will summarize content and overlook details, generating an incomplete analysis. This is troublesome in research since a "small", left out detail could actually be revolutionary to your work.
